Saying no, and the plan you keep

Five sessions of evidence say the same thing in different ways: estimates run short, a full week makes everything late, and interruptions eat the slack you left. Tonight is about the two moves that protect a plan from all three, handing work back with a real alternative and renegotiating a date before it is missed, and about the part of capacity that is refilled outside work. Then everyone builds one page: next week's plan, with a slack line drawn on it.

Part 1 · covers handing work back

Hand back with a date, not a shrug

5 min live

Saying no is the wrong frame for most work requests. The person asking usually needs the outcome, not you specifically and not necessarily today. A plain no leaves them stuck; a silent yes leaves your week overloaded and their task quietly late. The useful answer sits between the two: a decision made against the plan you already have, and, when the request does not fit, an alternative they can act on.

LiveThe three alternatives3 min▶

When a request does not fit and is not more important than what is already planned, hand it back with one of three alternatives. A later date: "I can do this Thursday", which is a yes with a real slot behind it. A smaller scope: "I can get you the top-level table today; the breakdown by region would take another day", which gives them the part they need most. Someone else: "the platform team owns this pipeline; I'll copy them in", which routes the work to the person whose week it belongs in.

This is practice, a working norm that experienced people use, not a result from a study we can cite. What makes it work is the step before it: the decision is taken against the plan, not against your mood. If the request is more important than something already planned, the honest answer is a swap, and a swap is only honest when you say out loud which item slips and to when. A yes that silently pushes something else late is a hand-back to a person who was never asked.

LiveWhat the bench says about handing back (modelled)2 min▶

Session 4's calendar bench offered the same 128 requests to four planning rules over 8 modelled weeks. Rung 3, planning to 70 percent of focus hours by estimate, handed back 36 of the 128 asks and missed 3.3 percent of deadlines. Rung 1, booking every hour, handed back 18 and missed 31.8 percent. Handing back twice as many asks bought a week where almost everything accepted arrived on time.

The honest other side is printed too. Saying yes to every ask, on rung 4, finished 107 tasks inside the 8 weeks against 90, by working 38.6 hours of overtime, and missed 42.2 percent of deadlines. So yes-to-everything does get more done, and it makes nearly half of it late. A hand-back is not a refusal to work; it is a choice to keep the dates on the work you accepted. These are modelled numbers from one seeded draw, not measurements of any real team.

Self-studyShort scripts for a lead and for a peer3 min▶

To a lead, make the trade visible and let them choose, because the priority call is theirs: "Happy to take the board slide. It doesn't fit this week as planned. If it goes first, the churn analysis moves from Thursday to next Tuesday. Or I can do it Monday. Which do you prefer?" You are not refusing; you are showing the price and asking the person who sets priorities to pay it knowingly.

To a peer, keep it warm and specific: "I can't get to it today. I could look Thursday morning, or if you only need the totals, I can send those by end of day. Would either help?" If someone else owns the work: "That one sits with the platform team; I'll introduce you to the person who knows it." Every script ends with a concrete option, never with "sorry, I'm swamped", which tells them your state and leaves them with nothing to do.

Real world

The analyst who always said yes. A familiar pattern on data teams: one analyst becomes the person everyone asks, because they never say no. For a few months it looks like generosity. Then their own planned work starts landing a week late, every week, and the people who asked first are now waiting behind the people who asked last. Nobody was refused anything; they were all quietly put in a queue. The day that analyst starts answering "Thursday, or the totals today", the asks do not stop, but the dates start holding.

Part 2 · covers renegotiating before a date is missed

Renegotiate early, once

5 min live

Every slipping task has a moment, usually well before the deadline, when you can already see it will not land. Speak up there and the person waiting still has options: a new date, a smaller version, a second pair of hands. Speak up at the deadline and there is only one thing left to offer. The difference is not how much work is left; it is how much room the other person still has to plan around it.

LiveAn early-warning rule3 min▶

The hard part of renegotiating early is knowing when "early" is. A simple rule removes the judgement call: when a task has used its estimate and is not near done, say so that day. Not at the end of the week, not once you have tried one more thing. That day, to the person waiting, with a new date and one option: "The churn analysis has used its six hours and I'm about half way. My new estimate is Tuesday. If you need something for Thursday's meeting, I can send the headline numbers without the segment breakdown."

And renegotiate once. A date that moves three times teaches the other person to stop believing your dates, which is worse than one honest slip. So the new date should be the one you will keep: use the "usual way" figure from session 2, not a fresh best guess. This rule is practice, a working norm, not a finding from a study; the evidence behind it is the planning fallacy itself, which says the first date was probably short.

Self-studySet the new date from your own ratio3 min▶

Session 2 gave you a median overrun ratio: actual time divided by estimate, over your last 5 to 10 tasks. Use it twice here. First, to set the original date: quote the best guess times the ratio, so fewer dates need renegotiating at all. Second, when a task does slip, estimate what is left and multiply that by your ratio too. If the remaining work looks like "about three hours", and your ratio is 1.2, say "about three and a half", and pick the date on that.

For work bigger than one person's week, a project with dependencies and several people, renegotiation becomes re-planning: critical paths, milestones and a steering conversation. That lives in Data PMO ↗. Here the unit is one task and one person waiting for it.

Real world

The Wednesday message that saved a Friday. An analyst realises on Wednesday that a model refresh will not be ready for Friday's review: the data had a gap that needs a backfill. They message the manager that afternoon: "Friday won't hold. I can show last month's model with a note, or move the review to Tuesday." The manager picks last month's model, tells the room in advance, and the review goes fine. The same news delivered on Friday morning would have been the same delay and a very different meeting.

Part 3 · covers recovery as part of capacity

Recovery is part of capacity

5 min live

A weekly plan usually counts hours available. It rarely counts what makes next week's hours usable. Research on recovery from work treats what happens outside working time as part of the same system: work uses something up, and some kinds of time away refill it. Here recovery is a planning input, things you can protect on the calendar, not a health topic. The body side of stress, breathing, sleep and physiology, is out of scope; the hub at Learn with Phoebe ↗ points to where it lives.

LiveThe four recovery experiences, in plain words3 min▶

Sabine Sonnentag and Charlotte Fritz (2007) built a questionnaire for how people recover from work and, in a sample of 930, distinguished four experiences by confirmatory factor analysis. Psychological detachment: being away from work in your head, not only in your body. Relaxation: low effort and low activation, chosen. Mastery: a challenge or a learning experience outside work. Control: deciding for yourself what to do with the time. This page relies on the paper's abstract; the full questionnaire items were not read here.

As planning inputs, they turn into small, concrete calendar decisions. Detachment means the Friday review ends with the list written down, so the work is somewhere other than your head. Control means at least one evening a week that nobody else has a claim on. Mastery and relaxation are yours to fill. None of this is a prescription; it is the honest observation that a plan which books every evening with catch-up work is planning next week with a smaller tank.

Self-studyRecovery can be trained, and control is latitude3 min▶

Sonnentag, Cheng and Parker (2022) review recovery research across breaks, evenings, weekends and vacations, and report that recovery can be trained. That review is reported here, not read: we cite its scope and its conclusion, not any effect size. The practical reading is modest: recovery is not only something that happens to you on holiday; short daily windows count, and the habits around them can be learned.

Robert Karasek (1979) gives the other half. He found that mental strain results from the interaction of job demands and job decision latitude, and that "the combination of low decision latitude and heavy job demands" goes with strain, in Swedish and US survey data. Tonight's moves are latitude. Choosing the order of your top three, proposing a later date, picking which item slips in a swap: each one is a decision you make about your own work, rather than demands arriving with no say in how they are met. The demands may be the same size; the strain picture changes when the control does.

Real world

The catch-up evenings. Someone falls behind in a busy month and fixes it the obvious way: two hours every evening, laptop on the sofa. The first week it works. By the third, the evening hours produce less, the morning hours start slower, and the backlog is about where it began. Nothing about their skill changed. They spent the refill on the drain. The fix that sticks usually looks smaller on paper: hand two things back, renegotiate one date, and keep the evenings for something that is not work.

Build-along

The weekly plan page

★ 22 min · everyone builds

One page, written on Monday, reviewed on Friday. Dara builds next week's on screen, from the numbers established across the six sessions; build yours alongside, on paper or in a note. Dara's page is constructed for teaching, and your numbers will differ. What should not differ is the shape: hours first, a plan level below them, a slack line nobody fills with new work, and a sentence ready for the day something slips.

Count your focus hours for the week. Start from the focus hours you found in session 1, multiply by the working days, and subtract the recurring work that happens whatever else does: reports, standing meetings, the weekly refresh. What is left is the time your list actually competes for.

★ Step 1 · focus hoursDara (constructed): focus hours a day about 6 x 5 days 30 h - recurring reports 2 h a day x 5 = 10 h = left for the list about 20 h Yours: ___ h a day x ___ days - ___ h recurring = ___ h

Set your plan level. Plan only part of those hours by estimate. Start at 70 percent. On session 4's bench, rung 3 accepted work worth 68.3 percent of focus hours by estimate, and once overruns, quick asks and resuming were counted it ran at a real load of 93.8 percent. Planning to 70 is not planning to be idle; it is planning for the week you will actually have.

★ Step 2 · plan levelleft for the list about 20 h x plan level 70% = planned work 14 h (by usual-way estimate) Bench, rung 3 (modelled): planned 68.3% by estimate, real load 93.8% once overruns and interruptions landed. Yours: ___ h x 70% = ___ h

Pick your top three from the list. Take them from the one sorted list you built in session 1, in order of consequence and date, and write each with its "usual way" hours from session 2. If the top three already overflow the planned hours, that is your first hand-back or renegotiation, found on Monday instead of Thursday.

★ Step 3 · top three1 quarterly churn analysis best 6 h -> usual way about 7 h 2 exec dashboard fix best 2 h -> about 2.5 h 3 ops data pulls (x3) best 2.5 h -> about 3 h total about 12.5 h of 14 Remaining 1.5 h: small lines from the list, in order.

Protect one block, or two. Give the important-not-urgent item a fixed slot that quick asks do not enter. Session 4's bench saved 4.6 hours of resuming over 8 weeks with a single daily focus block; session 5 showed why. Put it in the calendar where colleagues can see it, and batch the quick asks for after.

★ Step 4 · protected blockTue 9:00-12:00 churn analysis (status: focusing, back at 12) Thu 9:00-12:00 churn analysis 6 h protected; the last hour goes in Friday's slack if needed Quick asks during the block: noted, answered at 12:00 in one batch.

Draw the slack line, and write what may use it. Everything between your planned hours and your focus hours is slack, and it has a job: absorbing overruns, quick asks and the one urgent thing that will arrive. Write down that new work does not go below the line. When a request comes in, Part 1's decision tree asks whether it fits above the line, not whether there is technically an empty hour.

★ Step 5 · the slack line------------------------------------------- 14 h planned SLACK: about 6 h may use it: overruns on the top three quick asks (batched) one urgent job a day may NOT use: new requests -> decision tree ------------------------------------------- 20 h focus

Write the renegotiation sentence and the Friday review. Write the sentence now, while nothing is late, so it is ready on the day a task uses its estimate. Then set a 15-minute Friday slot: planned against done, and this week's overrun ratio added to your session 2 record. Next Monday's page starts from Friday's numbers.

★ Step 6 · sentence and reviewRenegotiation sentence: "[Task] has used its [N] hours and I'm about [half] way. New estimate: [day]. If you need something sooner, I can send [smaller version] by [day]." Friday review (15 min): planned ___ h done ___ h overrun ratio this week ___ (add it to your pairs) handed back ___ renegotiated ___ list written down, laptop closed.

Keep it to one page. The weekly plan earns its place by being quick to write and quick to check. If it grows past a page, it is turning into a project plan, which is a different tool. Same template every week, the same six boxes, filled in under twenty minutes on Monday and checked in fifteen on Friday.

Homework

For the week after

30 min
  1. Run the page for one full week. Write it Monday, keep it visible, and fill in the Friday review honestly, including what you planned and did not do.
  2. Hand back one request with an alternative. Use one of the three: a later date, a smaller scope, or someone else. Note what you offered and how it landed.
  3. Use the early-warning rule once. The first time a task uses its estimate and is not near done, send the renegotiation sentence that day. Record the original date, the new one, and whether the new one held.
  4. Protect one recovery window. Pick one evening or weekend block, decide which of the four experiences you want from it, and keep work out of it. On Friday, write one line on whether Monday felt different.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A request arrives that does not fit this week's plan and is not more important than anything already planned. What is the best response?

A hand-back with an alternative gives the asker something to act on and keeps the dates on work you already accepted. A silent swap pushes someone else's task late without asking them; a bare no leaves the asker stuck. On the bench (modelled), saying yes to every ask missed 42.2% of deadlines.

2 · When is the right moment to renegotiate a date?

Early leaves the other person options: a new date, a smaller version, help. At the deadline only an apology is left. And a date that moves three times teaches people to stop believing your dates, so set the new one from your overrun ratio. This rule is practice, not a cited finding.

3 · Which four recovery experiences did Sonnentag and Fritz (2007) distinguish?

The four were distinguished by confirmatory factor analysis in a sample of 930 (abstract read). Used here as planning inputs: what you protect on the calendar so next week starts with capacity to spend.
