Learn Time Management with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Busy is not the same as important

A full day feels productive, and a full day can be spent entirely on things that would not have mattered if they waited. Urgency is about the clock; importance is about what happens if the thing is late or never done. Tonight we separate the two, trace the famous urgent-important line to what was actually said in 1954, merge five scattered to-do lists into one list with one order, and read stress the way an operations person would: as load against capacity.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Dara is an invented new analyst, six weeks into a busy data team. Requests arrive all day: data pulls for the operations team, fixes to dashboards that broke overnight, reviews of a teammate's code, one genuine analysis for the quarterly planning cycle, and a steady drip of quick questions in team chat. Dara says yes to everything, works late most evenings, and still misses dates. Nothing Dara does is lazy. The week is simply planned against a capacity that does not exist, and every session here is a move that closes that gap.

Part 1 · covers urgent against important

The clock is loud, the consequence is quiet

5 min live

Every request carries two separate facts. How soon it is due is printed on it: "by end of day", "asap", a red badge in team chat. What happens if it is late is usually not printed anywhere, and you have to work it out. Because one fact shouts and the other has to be asked for, urgent work fills the day by default, and important work that is not yet due waits until it becomes urgent too.

LiveWhat Eisenhower actually said, and when3 min▶

On 19 August 1954, speaking at the Second Assembly of the World Council of Churches in Evanston, Illinois, President Eisenhower illustrated a point by "quoting the statement of a former college president": "I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent." The text is in the American Presidency Project's record of the address, and Quote Investigator's 2014 trace confirms the date and the attribution.

Two details matter. First, Eisenhower was quoting someone else, and he did not name them. Second, he described a dilemma, not a method: he went on to call it "a dilemma of modern man". There is no grid in the speech. The line is a sharp observation about how the two kinds of problem feel, and it is deliberately absolute; in a real week, some things are both.

Self-studyWhere the four boxes came from3 min▶

The two-by-two "time management matrix", with its famous Quadrant II for important but not urgent work, is Stephen Covey's: it appears in The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People (1989) and is developed in First Things First (1994, with A. Roger and Rebecca Merrill). Both attributions are reported here; the books were not read for this page. Quote Investigator says plainly that it does not know whether Eisenhower ever used a matrix himself.

So "the Eisenhower matrix" is half true: the line is Eisenhower's quotation, the matrix is Covey's construction decades later, and the name joined them afterwards. It is still a useful tool. Teaching it accurately costs one sentence, and it is a small first example of a habit every session here repeats: ask where a famous claim actually came from before you build on it.

Real world

The analysis that became a fire drill. A pattern on most data teams: a planning analysis is requested six weeks before a board meeting, sits behind daily requests because nothing about it is due this week, and is finally started nine days before the meeting, at which point it is urgent, important, and done in a rush with no time to check it. Nobody decided to do it badly. The clock decided the order, week after week, until the deadline was close enough to shout.

Part 2 · covers one priority list

Five lists are no list

5 min live

Dara's commitments live in five places: an email inbox, team chat, the team's ticket queue, notes from three meetings, and memory. Each place has its own order, usually newest first. When the commitments are scattered, the order Dara works in is set by whichever channel was opened last, which is the opposite of sorting by consequence.

LiveWhy one list beats a better list3 min▶

You cannot prioritise items you cannot see side by side. A chat ping and a ticket can only be compared when they sit on the same page with the same two facts written next to them: what happens if this is late, and when it is really due. That is the whole job of the list. The tool does not matter: a text file, a spreadsheet, a notebook page, or the task tool your team already uses, as long as it is one place and everything you have agreed to is on it.

Dara's first list had 23 lines on it, which was a surprise in itself. Seven of them had been agreed to in chat and were in nobody's ticket queue, so no one but Dara knew they existed, and Dara had forgotten three of them.

Self-studyCapture everything, decide once3 min▶

Capturing and deciding are two different moments. Capture is fast and indiscriminate: anything you said yes to, or might have, goes on the list with its source. Deciding happens once a day, or once a week for the plan, with the list in front of you. Mixing the two, deciding about each request as it arrives, is how the most recent request ends up first.

Finding out where your hours actually went last week is a separate exercise, a time audit, taught properly in AI Productivity ↗ under "Where your week actually goes". Here the list is about what you have promised; the audit is about what you did. They meet in session 6, when the plan for next week is checked against both.

Write the consequence in words, not stars. "VP presents wrong churn numbers on Thursday" sorts itself against "nobody notices". Three stars against two stars does not, and it drifts towards whoever asked most recently or most loudly.

Part 3 · covers stress as load against capacity

Stress, read as load against capacity

5 min live

A useful working definition for planning purposes: stress shows up when what is asked of you runs ahead of the time and the say you have to do it. Robert Karasek's 1979 job-strain study put the second half of that sentence on a firm footing: mental strain went with the combination of heavy job demands and low decision latitude, not with heavy demands alone.

LiveTurn a feeling into hours3 min▶

"I'm overwhelmed" is true and hard to act on. "I have 41 hours of agreed work due in the next two weeks and about 40 focus hours to do it in, before a single quick question arrives" is a planning problem a manager can help with: drop something, move a date, or move work to someone else. The list from Part 2, with an estimate on every line, is what turns the first sentence into the second.

The demand side is the list. The capacity side is not your working hours: it is the hours left for focused work after meetings, admin and the quick questions that will arrive whether you plan for them or not. Dara's team calendar leaves about six focus hours on a normal day. 41 hours against 40 looks nearly fine; session 3 shows why a plan that fills every focus hour makes everything late, and session 4 measures it.

Self-studyWhat the job-strain model does and does not say3 min▶

Karasek's paper, read at source, used national survey data from Sweden and the United States and reports that "it is the combination of low decision latitude and heavy job demands which is associated with mental strain". It is a model about jobs, and a correlational one: it does not tell you that a list will lower anyone's stress, and nothing on this page claims that. What it does support is a direction for the planning moves here: the levers that add say over order and dates are worth as much attention as the levers that reduce the amount of work.

Recovery, the other half of capacity, comes in session 6, with Sonnentag and Fritz's four recovery experiences read at source. The physiology of stress is out of scope here and belongs to planned courses on the shelf.

Real world

The new joiner who cannot say which is first. New analysts often have the least say of anyone on a team: they do not yet know which requests can wait, so every request is treated as the most important one. A short weekly check with a lead, "here is my list in order, is the order right?", hands over the prioritising decision explicitly until the new person can make it. It costs ten minutes and gives back exactly the latitude Karasek's model points at.

Build-along

One list for Dara's week, then for yours

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Dara sits down on a Monday morning with every source open and builds one list. Build yours alongside it, for a real week of your own; if you would rather not use your own, use Dara's. Six steps, one sheet, and by the end every line has a consequence, a real date, an estimate and a decision.

Collect every commitment, from every source. Open each place in turn and copy anything you have agreed to, or might have, onto one sheet with where it came from. Do not sort yet and do not decide anything.

★ Step 1 · collectSources, in order: email · team chat · ticket queue · last week's meeting notes · memory One line each: task | where it came from | who asked Dara's count: 23 lines (7 from chat that were in no queue)

Write what happens if each one is late. In words, about a person or a decision. If you cannot name a consequence, write "not stated" and treat that as a question for the person who asked.

★ Step 2 · cost if lateexec dashboard VP shows wrong churn numbers on Thursday ops data pull ops team waits a day for its weekly numbers deck reformat nobody notices chat "one number" not stated -> ask

Give each line a real date. A date someone depends on, not a feeling. "asap", "when you can" and "end of week" are not dates until you have asked what they are attached to.

★ Step 3 · real datesHard date: tied to a meeting, a release or a person waiting Soft date: someone's preference; can usually move if asked No date: write "none"; it is still a commitment until dropped "asap" -> "Is this for the 3 pm review, or for later this week?"

Estimate each line in hours. Quickly, and expect to be wrong in one direction: session 2 shows that most people underestimate, and how to correct for it. For now, write the number you believe.

★ Step 4 · estimatesRound to 30 min. Add up the lines due in the next two weeks. Dara: 41 h of agreed work due within two weeks 6 focus hours a day x 10 days = 60 h of focus time recurring reports, 2 h a day = 20 h already spoken for left for the list = 40 h, before any quick ask

Sort by consequence first, date second, and decide each line. Do now, this week, protect a block, hand back, drop, or ask. Important work that is not due yet gets a protected block now, so it never becomes the fire drill from Part 1.

★ Step 5 · decidedo now due today and costly if late this week due within the week, real consequence protect a block important, not yet due (the mint box) hand back real, but not yours or not this fortnight drop, say so no consequence anyone could name ask no consequence or no date stated

Write the gap as a sentence a manager can act on. Hours of agreed work against focus hours available, and the two or three lines you propose to move. This is the sentence session 6 turns into a renegotiation.

★ Step 6 · the gap, in hours"I have about 41 hours of agreed work due in the next two weeks and about 40 focus hours, before any quick asks. I propose moving the deck reformat (drop) and the March backfill (to the 21st). Can you confirm the churn analysis is the priority?"

Keep the list where you will see it tomorrow. A list built once and never opened again is a nice afternoon. The habit that matters is the two-minute look at the top of the day: what changed overnight, what moved into "do now", and whether the protected block is still protected.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Finish your list. Every source, every line, with cost if late, a real date and an estimate. Count the lines and note how many were in no system but your head.
  2. Ask about one "asap". Find one request with no real date and ask what it is attached to. Write down what you learned.
  3. Protect one block. Pick one important, not-yet-urgent line and put a block for it in next week's calendar.
  4. Keep your estimates. Next to three of this week's lines, record how long each actually took. Session 2 starts from those three pairs of numbers.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which statement about the urgent-important idea is accurate?

The American Presidency Project records the 19 August 1954 Evanston address with the line attributed to "a former college president". The matrix is reported as Covey's (1989, 1994), and Quote Investigator does not know whether Eisenhower ever used one.

2 · Dara keeps commitments in email, chat, the ticket queue and memory. What is the main problem with that?

Prioritising means comparing, and you can only compare items you can see side by side with the same two facts written next to them. Checking five sources daily still lets the newest message set the order. Chat requests can be very important; seven of Dara's were in no queue at all.

3 · Karasek's 1979 job-strain study is best summarised as:

The paper reports that "the combination of low decision latitude and heavy job demands" is associated with strain. It is correlational survey evidence about jobs; it does not show that a list lowers anyone's stress, which is why the page claims only a direction for planning.
