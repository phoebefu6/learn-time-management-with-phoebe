Interruptions and the cost of switching

On session 4's bench, one protected morning block saved Dara 4.6 hours of getting back into interrupted work over eight weeks. That 10-minute resume cost was an assumption. Tonight we look at what has actually been measured: switch costs of tenths of a second in the lab, a 40 percent figure that turns out to be a quoted estimate, people who got faster under interruption and paid in stress, and office workers who took about 25 minutes on average to return to interrupted work. Part 1 · covers task switching in the lab and the 40 percent claim

Tenths of a second in the lab, 40 percent in the quote

5 min live

"Multitasking costs you 40 percent of your productivity" is on posters, slides and a great many posts, usually credited to a 2001 study. The 2001 study is real and careful, and it does not contain the number. Follow the claim back one step at a time and you can see exactly where a lab measurement turned into a quoted estimate, and then into something that sounds like a fact.

LiveWhat the experiments measured3 min▶

Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer and Jeffrey Evans ran four experiments in which people either alternated between different tasks or did the same task repeatedly, and timed how much slower the switches were. Switching took longer, and the extra time grew when the rules of the new task were more complex and shrank when people were cued about which task came next. The two effects added up rather than interacting, which the authors read as support for two stages of control: shifting the goal, then activating the new task's rules.

The units matter. The APA's own 2001 press release on the paper describes rule activation as taking "several tenths of a second". That is a cost per switch, measured in a lab, on simple tasks. A text search of the full paper for "40%", "40 percent", "percent of" and "productive" finds nothing. Whatever the 40 percent is, it is not a result of these four experiments.

Self-studyHow to say the 40 percent honestly3 min▶

The figure is reported to come from an APA page titled "Multitasking: Switching costs" (2006), which says Meyer has estimated that shifting between tasks "can cost as much as 40 percent of someone's productive time". That page sits behind a bot wall and could not be read directly, so the wording here is reported, not checked. Three things are worth keeping straight: it is an estimate, it is a ceiling ("as much as"), and it is one researcher's judgement quoted in an article, not a measured outcome. How the estimate was derived is not stated in anything read for this page.

An honest sentence you can reuse: "David Meyer, one of the authors of a 2001 task-switching study, has estimated, as quoted by the APA, that switching can cost as much as 40 percent of productive time. The experiments themselves measured costs of tenths of a second per switch." That sentence is longer than the poster, and every part of it is true.

Real world

The slide at the all-hands. A department head opens a "focus time" initiative with a slide: "Multitasking costs 40% of productivity (Meyer, 2001)". An analyst in the room goes looking for the table the 40 lives in, and cannot find it, because there is none. The initiative may still be a good idea. The slide just made a claim the paper does not make, and the analyst who checks is now less likely to trust the next number on it. Quote the estimate as an estimate, and the initiative loses nothing.

Part 2 · covers interruptions at work

Faster, and more stressed

5 min live

If the lab says a switch costs a fraction of a second, why does an interrupted day feel so expensive? Gloria Mark and her colleagues studied interruptions in work-like settings and found something less obvious than "interruptions slow you down". In a controlled experiment, interrupted people finished sooner. They paid for it in a different currency.

LivePeople compensate by working faster, at a price3 min▶

Mark, Gudith and Klocke (2008) gave 48 participants a work task and interrupted some of them. Interrupted people finished in 20.31 minutes (interruptions on the same topic) or 20.60 minutes (a different topic), against 22.77 minutes with no interruptions, and the quality of their work was no different. In their words, "people compensate for interruptions by working faster, but this comes at a price: experiencing more stress, higher frustration, time pressure and effort."

On a 1 to 20 scale, stress went from 6.92 at baseline to 9.46 and 9.13 when interrupted, and the authors report the rise after only about 20 minutes of interrupted work. Whether the interruption was on the same topic or a different one made no real difference. So the cost of an interrupted hour may not show up in the output at all. It shows up in the person, and in how long they can keep working that way. The body side of stress, sleep and recovery is for the courses linked from the hub ↗; here it is a workload signal.

Self-studyIn the field: 25 minutes to get back, with a huge spread4 min▶

Three years earlier, Mark, Gonzalez and Harris (2005) observed 24 information workers in detail. They counted work in "working spheres", units of work bigger than a single task, roughly a project or a responsibility. 57 percent of working spheres were interrupted. 77.2 percent of interrupted work was resumed on the same day, and resuming took on average 25 minutes 26 seconds, with a standard deviation of 54 minutes 48 seconds. Before getting back, people worked in an average of 2.26 other spheres. Left alone, they spent about 11 minutes in a sphere before switching.

Read the spread before the average. A standard deviation more than twice the mean says most returns were much quicker than 25 minutes and a few took hours; the average is pulled up by the long ones. It also measures time until the person came back, not time lost: much of those 25 minutes went on other real work in those 2.26 other spheres.

The figure that circulates widely, "23 minutes and 15 seconds to get back on task", is not in the 2005 paper or the 2008 paper; both were checked. It is attributed to a 2008 magazine interview with Mark that could not be opened, so it could not be traced to a paper. If you need a number, use 25 minutes 26 seconds from 2005, and say what it measured.

Four measurements, never one number. Tenths of a second is the cost of one switch between simple lab tasks. 40 percent is an estimate, quoted, of a ceiling on lost productive time. 25 minutes 26 seconds is how long office workers took, on average and with a huge spread, to come back to interrupted work while doing other work in between. Faster-but-more-stressed is what happened to output and to people over about 20 minutes of interruption in a lab. They answer different questions, and the literature has not combined them into one cost. Neither should a slide.

Real world

The most responsive person on the team. Every data team has one: answers chat within a minute, reruns the pull before lunch, always seems to get their own work done too. Their reviews say "reliable". What the reviews do not see is that they are compressing their planned work into the gaps, at a pace they could not keep up for a quarter, and that the important-not-urgent analysis has been "nearly done" for six weeks. Fast and fine on the outside, stretched on the inside: the 2008 result, at desk scale.

Part 3 · covers batching and protected blocks

Batch the quick asks

5 min live

You cannot make the asks stop, and most of them are legitimate. What you can change is when they cut in. Batching holds the quick asks until a set time and answers them together, so a planned task is broken once instead of four times. The research above does not give one number for what that saves; session 4's bench gives a modelled one, under an assumption it states.

LiveWhat the session 4 bench says a block saves3 min▶

On the bench, rung 3 plans to 70 percent of focus hours and lets every quick ask interrupt at once. Rung 4 is the same plan with one change: in the first three focus hours of each day, quick asks wait and are answered as one batch. Over the modelled eight weeks, resume overhead falls from 16.8 hours to 12.2, a saving of 4.6 hours, and deadlines missed fall from 3.3 to 1.1 percent. Nothing about intake changed; only when the interruptions landed.

Hold that number loosely, for the reason Part 2 gives. The bench charges 10 minutes to get back into interrupted work, and that is an assumption, because no paper read here gives one figure for it. The slider on session 4 shows how much rides on it: on rung 3 the mean wait is 5.1 hours with no resume cost, 5.7 at 10 minutes and 7.0 at 20. The direction is robust; the size depends on a number you should measure on yourself, which is what the build-along starts.

Self-studyA block a team will respect3 min▶

A block only works if other people can plan around it, so it needs three parts. Office hours for asks: a named time, every day, when quick asks get answered, so waiting has an end the asker can see. Visible status: the block is in the shared calendar and in your chat status, so nobody has to guess whether you are ignoring them. An escape hatch: one named channel for work that genuinely cannot wait, with a short definition of what counts.

The escape hatch is not a weakness in the rule; it is what makes the rule believable. On session 4's bench, the break button adds one urgent job a day that jumps every queue, focus block included. On rung 4 it lifts deadlines missed from 1.1 to 7.6 percent. A plan with a block still has to leave room for the one thing a day that really will not wait, and the block is what keeps the other, waitable asks from eating that room too. For meetings that cut into the block, the craft of running fewer and better ones is in Facilitation ↗.

Real world

Focus mornings that lasted a week. A team agrees on "no pings before noon". By Thursday it has quietly dissolved: a stakeholder pinged anyway about a real problem, someone answered, and after that every ping felt like it might be the real one. The version that lasts has the same mornings plus two additions: a daily 12:00 slot where every waiting ask is answered, and a single phone number for "production is down". Pings did not stop. They just stopped being a guess.

Build-along

An interruption log and a batching rule

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Dara kept a log for two working days: every time something broke into a planned task, one line. The log is constructed for teaching, and it turns a vague "I never get anything done" into a count you can act on. Build yours alongside; if you have not logged yet, follow Dara's sheet tonight and start your own two days tomorrow. Your numbers will differ.

Log two working days. Keep a note open and add one line every time something breaks into a planned task: a message you answer, a person at your desk, a call you join. Write the time, who it came from, what they asked and roughly how long it took. Do not judge anything yet; just count. If you want the full picture of where every hour goes, the time audit in AI Productivity ↗ goes wider; this log only tracks the breaks.

★ Step 1 · log two daysOne line per interruption: time | from | what they asked | minutes Dara (constructed), first three lines: Tue 9:20 team lead which number for the deck? 5 Tue 10:05 sales ops rerun yesterday's data pull 10 Tue 10:40 chat is the dashboard down? 5

Count and classify. At the end of the two days, mark each line with one question: could this have waited two or three hours with no real harm? Be honest in both directions; a broken executive number is not waitable, and a "quick question" that could have been a message usually is. Then count the total, the waitable ones and when they arrived.

★ Step 2 · count and classifyCould it have waited 2-3 hours with no real harm? yes / no Dara (constructed): interruptions in 2 days 14 could have waited 10 needed an answer soon 4 arrived before noon 9 Your numbers will differ.

Choose your block hours. Pick a block of two to three hours for your most important planned work, and one or two short office-hour slots when waiting asks get answered. Put the block where your hardest work goes best, and where the waitable asks are clustering, because those are the breaks it will remove. Dara's important-not-urgent churn analysis is about 6 hours of work, so two mornings of block move it forward visibly.

★ Step 3 · block hoursDara (constructed): block 09:00 - 12:00 planned work only office hours 12:00 - 12:30 every waiting ask answered 16:30 - 17:00 second sweep 9 of 14 asks arrived before noon -> the block is in the morning.

Write the rule and the escape hatch. One sentence for the rule, one for what breaks it. The escape hatch should name the kinds of work that genuinely cannot wait, and the one channel to use for them. Keep the list short: if everything qualifies, nothing is batched.

★ Step 4 · rule and escape hatchRule: From 9:00 to 12:00 I work on planned tasks. Asks that arrive then are answered at 12:00, in the order they came. Escape hatch (call or mention me directly): - a production dashboard or pipeline is down - a number already sent to an executive is wrong - anything the team lead marks urgent

Announce it. A block nobody knows about is just you not answering. Tell the people who ask most often, put the block in the shared calendar, and set a chat status. Lead with when they will get an answer, not with what you are protecting.

★ Step 5 · the announcement"Trying something for the next two weeks: I'm heads-down on planned work 9 to 12. Anything you send in that window, I'll answer at 12, usually within half an hour. If a dashboard is down or an exec number is wrong, call me or @ me and I'll jump on it straight away. Thanks for bearing with me."

Review after a week. Keep logging, then count again. How many asks broke the block, and how many of those were escape-hatch work? Did anyone wait in a way that caused harm? How far did the important-not-urgent item move? Adjust the hours or the escape-hatch list, not the idea, unless the count says the idea is not working.

★ Step 6 · one-week reviewAfter one week: asks that broke the block __ of those, escape-hatch work __ anyone harmed by waiting? yes / no, who, what hours on the churn analysis __ of about 6 change for next week hours / list / nothing Compare with your two-day baseline. Your numbers will differ.

Measure your own resume cost. While you log, note for a few interruptions when you were properly back in the task. Session 4's bench assumed 10 minutes; your log will give you a number of your own, and the spread will probably be wide, just as it was in the 2005 field study.

A text search of the 2001 paper finds no 40 percent, and the 2001 press release speaks of "several tenths of a second" with no percentage. The figure is Meyer's estimate of "as much as" 40 percent, quoted by the APA in 2006, which is reported here because that page could not be read directly.
