Part 0

Where we start

Nothing on this bench happened. Dara is constructed and the eight weeks are a model: the same seeded stream of requests every time, so the only thing that changes between rungs is how Dara plans. That makes the bench good for comparing ways of planning a week, and useless for predicting yours. Every number carries a "modelled" tag, and an independent Python run reproduces every default number on this page exactly.

Part 1 · covers how the model fills Dara's week

An intake gate, a queue and six focus hours

6 min live

Requests arrive through the working day. Each one passes an intake gate where Dara decides, by estimate, whether it fits the week's plan; if not, it goes back to the team lead to re-plan. Accepted work waits in a queue ordered by deadline. Dara works it for six focus hours a day, quick asks interrupt at once, and every interruption costs a few minutes to get back into the task.

LiveThe overrun table, and why it is mild3 min▶

Each request's true time is its estimate multiplied by an overrun drawn from twenty equally likely values, from 80 percent of the estimate to 165 percent. Six of the twenty are at or under 100 percent, so 30 percent of tasks finish within their estimate. That share is set to match Buehler, Griffin and Ross's thesis study, where 29.7 percent of students finished by their best estimate.

The tail is deliberately far milder than that study, where the average thesis took 55.5 days against a forecast of 33.9. Dara's tasks are hours, not months. In this particular draw, the 128 requests add up to 270 hours by estimate and 322 hours of true work, and 33 of the 128 come in at or under their estimate. Your own overruns could be larger or smaller; session 2's homework measures them.

Self-studyEvery rule the model follows4 min▶

Arrivals. Four kinds of request on a seeded stream, about one every 125 working minutes: data pulls (40 percent, due the next day), dashboard fixes (30 percent, due in two days), analyses (15 percent, 4 to 8 hours, due in five days) and reviews (15 percent, due the next day). Quick asks of 5 or 10 minutes arrive about every two focus hours, 132 of them in eight weeks.

Intake. Dara plans each week by estimate. A request is accepted if the estimates already accepted that week plus this one stay inside the plan level; otherwise it is handed back to the team lead to re-plan. The plan ignores last week's carry-over, the way most weekly plans do.

Work. Earliest deadline first; a planned task is never pre-empted by another planned task. Quick asks interrupt at once, and returning to the interrupted task costs a resume overhead, 10 minutes by default. The slider moves it from 0 to 20, because no paper gives one number for it (session 5). At the end of a day Dara keeps going for up to two hours on work due that day or overdue.

Scoring. Wait is the time from a request arriving to it being done, in focus hours (six focus hours is one working day). A deadline is missed if the work finishes after the end of its due day. Real load is all true work taken on, plus quick asks, urgent jobs and resume overheads, divided by the 240 focus hours in eight weeks.

Real world

Why the gate uses estimates. Nobody plans a week with true durations; nobody has them yet. Every sprint board, every calendar block and every "can you fit this in?" is decided on an estimate, and the work then takes what it takes. Software teams that track planned against delivered points see the same gap every sprint. The model keeps that honest: the decision is made on the number you have, the clock runs on the number you did not.

Build-along · the bench

Eight weeks of Dara, rung by rung

★ 26 min · the main event

Start on rung 1 and read the verdict line, then the eight numbers, then the backlog chart. Move one rung at a time and say out loud which number moved and which way. Only then try the two red buttons, one at a time and then together. The slider changes the resume overhead; every number quoted on this page is at the default of 10 minutes.

Read it in this order. Verdict, real load, deadlines missed, then mean wait. On rung 1 Dara takes on 110 of 128 asks, plans 92.8 percent of focus hours by estimate, runs a real load of 125.0 percent, misses 31.8 percent of deadlines and works 32.8 hours of overtime. Keep those numbers in your head as you climb.

Part 2 · covers reading the ladder

Seventy on paper, ninety-four in the week

6 min live

Each step down the ladder plans less of the week. The real load falls with it, but never as far as the plan: the gap between the two is the overrun on accepted work plus the quick asks and the minutes spent getting back into interrupted tasks. Slack is where those go.

LiveWhy 70 percent is not idle3 min▶

On rung 3 Dara plans 68.3 percent of focus hours by estimate and the week runs at 93.8 percent. Three things fill the difference: the accepted work turns out bigger than estimated, quick asks arrive whether planned or not, and every interruption costs a few minutes to get back into the task. None of that slack is spare time in the sense of nothing to do; it is the room the unplanned work needs.

The result is the one session 3 predicted: deadlines missed fall from 31.8 percent on rung 1 to 3.3 percent on rung 3, mean wait from 13.6 to 5.7 focus hours, and the backlog left at the end of week 8 from 33.6 hours to 3.8. Overtime falls from 32.8 hours to 4.1.

LiveTwo numbers at once: rungs 1 and 2 finish the same work3 min▶

Rung 1 takes on 110 asks; rung 2 takes on 105. Both finish 101 tasks inside the eight weeks. The five extra asks rung 1 accepts do not get done sooner; they sit in the queue and push everything behind them later. Rung 1 misses 31.8 percent of deadlines against 8.6, and works 32.8 hours of overtime against 4.9. Booking the last 15 percent bought nothing finished, only lateness.

This is the two-number read that matters on any plan: tasks finished and promises kept, side by side. A plan that looks fuller on Monday can finish the same amount by Friday with far more of it late.

Self-studyWhat slack costs: 90 tasks against 1013 min▶

Rung 3 is not free. Dara hands back 36 asks instead of 18 and finishes 90 tasks inside the eight weeks instead of 101. Somebody else does that work, or it waits, or it is dropped. The model does not pretend otherwise. What rung 3 buys is that 89 of its 92 accepted tasks meet their date, so the people waiting on Dara can plan around her.

The focus block on rung 4 changes nothing about intake; it only batches the quick asks that arrive in the first three focus hours. Resume overhead falls from 16.8 hours to 12.2 over the eight weeks, a saving of 4.6 hours, and deadlines missed go from 3.3 to 1.1 percent: three late tasks become one. At a load in the nineties, a few hours matter more than they look, which is the queueing curve's whole point.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever, the break and the curve

Say yes, add one urgent job, and check the theory

6 min live

The red buttons are there to make the bench report a worse number. If no input could make the week worse, the bench would be measuring nothing. Then the last chart on the widget sets the whole model beside Kingman's approximation from session 3, the queueing formula for how waiting grows with load.

LiveThe anti-lever: say yes to every ask3 min▶

The obvious fix for a team that keeps asking is to take everything. Turn the first red button on with rung 4 selected and the intake cap disappears: all 128 asks are accepted, 112.5 percent of focus hours by estimate. Real load goes to 145.8 percent, mean wait from 5.4 to 22.4 focus hours, deadlines missed from 1.1 to 42.2 percent, overtime from 2.3 to 38.6 hours, and 78.3 hours of accepted work are still waiting at the end of week 8. With no cap, rungs 1 to 3 become the same week, because the cap was the only thing that made them different.

The honest nuance, printed on the widget too: saying yes does finish more inside the eight weeks, 107 tasks against 90 on rung 4, by working 38.6 hours of overtime. The extra output is real, and so is the fact that 42.2 percent of everything Dara promised is late. That is the trade, in two numbers.

LiveThe break: one urgent interrupt a day3 min▶

The second red button adds a 30 to 60 minute urgent job at a seeded minute every day and lets it jump every queue, focus block included. On rung 1 deadlines missed rise from 31.8 to 55.5 percent and overtime to 50.3 hours. On rung 4 they rise from 1.1 to 7.6 percent and overtime to 8.1 hours. Both rungs feel the same interruption; only one of them had room for it.

With both red buttons on rung 4, deadlines missed reach 70.3 percent. The break is the bench's proof that it measures something: put load in, and the numbers move the way session 3 said they would.

Self-studyHow far the model agrees with Kingman, and where it does not4 min▶

The right-hand chart runs a plain first-come-first-served queue with exactly Dara's task mix, 100,000 tasks at each of eight loads, and plots the average wait before work starts. Kingman's approximation, read in Technion lecture notes that credit his 1961 paper, predicts that wait from three numbers: the load, the variability of arrivals and of task times, and the average task time (2.2 hours here). The two agree within 0.4 hours up to 91 percent load: 13.0 hours against 12.7 at 85.7 percent, 22.5 against 22.1 at 91.3 percent. At 95.6 percent the model waits 41.4 hours and Kingman says 46.4; the lecture notes describe the formula as an upper bound in general and exact only in heavy traffic, which is consistent with that gap.

One difference you should notice: at the same load, the eight-week bench shows much shorter waits than the curve. Rung 3 runs at 93.8 percent and waits 5.7 hours on average, where the curve says tens of hours. The bench starts from an empty desk and stops after eight weeks, and its deadline ordering serves short, urgent work first; the curve is the long run. A busy month that feels fine is often a queue that has not caught up with its load yet.

  2. Count your quick asks. For two days, tally every interruption that pulled you off a planned task. Session 5 starts from that count.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On rung 3 Dara plans 68.3 percent of focus hours by estimate. Why does the modelled week run at 93.8 percent?

The plan is made on estimates; the week runs on true time plus unplanned work. Rung 3 accepts fewer asks (92 against 110), and Dara's speed is the same on every rung. The slack is where the overrun and the interruptions go.

2 · Rung 1 accepts 110 asks and rung 2 accepts 105. What does the bench show about the difference?

Above full load, extra asks do not get done sooner; they queue and push everything later. Read tasks finished and deadlines kept side by side and the last 15 percent of booking buys only lateness.

3 · The eight-week bench waits far less than Kingman's curve at the same load. Which explanation fits?

On the long-run plain queue the model and Kingman agree within 0.4 hours up to 91 percent load. The bench is a short, deadline-ordered stretch that has not reached steady state, which is also why a busy month can feel fine before the queue catches up.
