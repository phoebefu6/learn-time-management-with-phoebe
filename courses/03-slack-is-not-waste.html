Slack is not waste

Part 1 · covers utilisation and waiting

A full week is a queue

5 min live

Operations researchers have a model for a single counter that serves one customer at a time while others line up. It fits a knowledge worker surprisingly well. Requests arrive when they arrive, not on a schedule. You serve one at a time. While you are busy, new work does not disappear; it waits. The quiet hours are when the line gets worked down, and if there are no quiet hours, the line only grows.

LiveUtilisation for one person3 min▶

Utilisation is the share of your available focus time that is spent busy on work. For one person: busy focus time divided by focus time available. If Dara has 20 focus hours a week for the list and spends 16 of them working on it, utilisation is 80 percent. The other 4 hours are not idle in any useful sense of the word; they are the gaps in which a bunch of arrivals gets worked down.

Two things make this number slippery. First, it is about the hours you actually spent, not the hours you planned: a plan at 70 percent can run at 100 once estimates overrun. Second, it says nothing about how long work waits. Two weeks can both be 80 percent busy, and in one the requests trickled in evenly while in the other they arrived in clumps. The second week makes work wait much longer. Part 2 puts numbers on that.

Self-studyLittle's law: the line, the rate and the wait3 min▶

One relation ties the length of a queue to how long things wait in it. Little's law, read in the MIT OpenCourseWare 2.854 M/M/1 note, is L = lambda W: the average number of items in the system (L) equals the rate at which they arrive (lambda) times the average time each spends there (W). It holds on average over a long run, whatever the pattern of arrivals or task lengths, which is why it is so useful on a messy desk.

Turn it round and it gives you a wait you can estimate from two counts you already have. Constructed arithmetic: if about 10 tasks sit on your list on a typical day, and you finish about 2.5 a day (in a steady week that is also the rate they arrive), the average task spends about 10 / 2.5 = 4 days on your list. Nobody needs to time anything. If the stakeholder's expectation is two days, the list is twice as long as it can be, whatever the estimates say. The same note gives the exact wait for the simplest random queue, Wq = lambda / (mu(mu - lambda)), where mu is the rate you can finish work; Part 2 turns that into a curve.

Real world

"It only takes ten minutes, why is it still not done?" A familiar complaint on data teams: a stakeholder asks for a quick table change, it would take ten minutes, and it ships four days later. Nobody was slow. The analyst spent every hour of those four days on other work, all of it also waiting. The ten minutes was the service time. The four days was the queue, and the queue is set by how full the analyst's week is, not by the size of the request.

Part 2 · covers the queueing curve

Waiting grows slowly, then explodes

5 min live

For the simplest random queue, one server with arrivals and task lengths both random in the standard way, the average wait before service starts, measured in task-lengths, is rho / (1 - rho), where rho is utilisation. At half busy you wait about one task-length. At 80 percent you wait four. At 95 percent you wait nineteen. The curve is flat for a long time and then turns almost vertical, and most full calendars live on the steep part.

LiveFrom 80 to 90 percent, the wait more than doubles3 min▶

Read the curve in pairs. From 50 to 80 percent busy, utilisation rises by 30 points and the wait goes from 1 task-length to 4. From 80 to 90 percent, it rises by only 10 points and the wait goes from 4 to 9, more than double. From 90 to 95, five more points take it from 9 to 19. Each step toward full costs more waiting than the last, and at 100 percent there is no ceiling at all: a desk that is always busy never catches up on a bunch.

In hours: if Dara's typical task is about two hours, an 80 percent week means a new request waits about eight hours before Dara even starts it, roughly two working days of list time. Push the week to 90 percent and the same request waits about eighteen. Nothing about Dara's speed changed. The values 1, 4 and 19 are the worked values printed on Mandelbaum's Technion slide; 9 comes from the same formula, rho / (1 - rho) = 0.9 / 0.1. Use 9: the slide's own figure at 90 percent does not match its formula.

Self-studyKingman's approximation: variability makes it worse4 min▶

Real work is rarely the tidy random queue. Mandelbaum's Technion lecture notes, "The G/G/1 Queue", give the general approximation for one server with any arrival pattern and any spread of task lengths: average wait is about E(S) x rho / (1 - rho) x (C2(A) + C2(S)) / 2. E(S) is the average task length. C2(A) and C2(S) are the squared coefficients of variation of the gaps between arrivals and of the task lengths: how uneven each one is, relative to its average. The notes label the formula "Exact for M/G/1", an "Upper bound in general" and "Asymptotically exact as rho -> 1", and credit the heavy-traffic result to Kingman (1961). The 1961 paper itself was not read for this page; the formula is as printed in the notes.

The variability term is the part that matters for a working week. With both C2 values at 1 it collapses to the curve above. Anything that makes task lengths more uneven raises C2(S), and overruns do exactly that: a list where most tasks land on estimate but one in five runs to 1.5 times or 2 times has a wider spread of true lengths than its estimates suggest. Wider spread, longer wait, at the same utilisation. Clumped arrivals do the same through C2(A). Session 4 checks the bench against Kingman: agreement within 0.4 hours up to 91.3 percent load.

Real world

The team that was always "nearly caught up". A reporting team proudly runs at near full capacity: every analyst booked, no idle afternoons. Their turnaround on routine requests drifts from two days to a week over a quarter, and the team lead hires a contractor to "add capacity". The contractor adds only a little capacity, a day or so a week. Turnaround improves by far more than that little extra would suggest. That is the curve read from the other side: taking a desk from the steep part back to the flat part buys a lot of waiting back for a little capacity.

Part 3 · covers buffers and Parkinson's law

Put the buffer where the variance is

5 min live

If slack is what absorbs overruns and bunches, the question is where to put it. The habit most people reach for is padding every estimate a little. The alternative is one shared block of unbooked time that whichever task runs long can use. Same hours, very different behaviour. And the objection everyone raises, "work expands to fill the time", turns out to come from a piece of satire, which is worth knowing before you let it decide your plan.

LiveOne shared buffer beats padding every task3 min▶

Padding each task spreads your slack evenly across the list, but overruns are not even. Session 2's ratios showed most of Dara's tasks landing near estimate and a few running long. Per-task padding gives every task a small cushion, most of which goes unused, and still leaves the one task that runs badly without enough. Pool the same hours into one unbooked block, late in the week or late in the day, and it can absorb whichever task overruns, however far.

This is planning practice, not a finding from a study read for this page; treat it as a design to test on your own week. The reasoning is the queueing one from Part 2: a shared block keeps your booked hours on the flat part of the curve while leaving room for the spread. Two habits make it work. Name the block on your calendar so it does not get given away, and when it goes unused, spend it on the important-not-urgent item, Dara's quarterly churn analysis, rather than on new asks.

Self-studyWhat Parkinson's law actually was3 min▶

"Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." The line opens C. Northcote Parkinson's essay in The Economist of 19 November 1955, as reported; the original page could not be fetched for this page. The essay was satire about civil-service headcount: officials multiply subordinates and create work for one another, regardless of how much real work there is. It was not a study of individual people, their schedules or their slack.

So here is the disagreement to hold. Many people quote the line as proof that unbooked time gets wasted, and the experience behind that is real: an open afternoon can drift. The queueing evidence in Part 2 says the opposite about a full week: without slack, every wait grows, and fast. Neither cancels the other. The line is a sharp joke about organisations, not evidence about you, and the honest response to drift is a named slack block with a default use, not a calendar booked to every hour.

Real world

The Friday afternoon that saved Tuesday. An analyst keeps Friday afternoons unbooked and tells the team lead why: "That is where this week's overruns go." Most weeks something lands in it: a dashboard fix that ran long on Tuesday, a quick ask that turned into a half day. When nothing does, the analyst spends it on the long-term model nobody is chasing. Over a quarter the analyst's dates hold more often than anyone else's, and the long-term model ships. The block was never empty time; it was the part of the plan built for the week as it actually runs.

Build-along

Find your real load

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Your plan says how full your week looks by estimate. Your real load says how full it runs: estimates times your overrun ratio, plus the quick asks and the minutes spent getting back into work after each interruption. Build Dara's constructed week first, then your own next week beside it. Your numbers will differ.

Count the focus hours left for your list. Start from your focus hours in a typical week, then take out the recurring work that happens whatever is on the list: standing reports, regular check-ins, the weekly refresh. What is left is the capacity your list actually competes for. If you have not measured where your week goes, the time audit in AI Productivity ↗ is the way to get the first number.

★ Step 1 · capacity for the listDara (constructed): focus hours a day 6 h recurring reports a day -2 h left for the list a day 4 h x 5 days = 20 h a week You: ____ h a day - ____ h recurring = ____ h x 5 = ____ h

Sum the planned estimates for next week. Take the lines you intend to work on next week and add up your best-guess estimates, the first number from session 2's two-number habit. Do not add any padding here; the next steps do that with your own data.

★ Step 2 · planned by estimateDara's lines for next week, best guesses: quarterly churn analysis, first half 3.0 h exec dashboard fix 2.0 h ops data pulls (x4) 4.0 h code reviews (x3) 3.0 h analysis section for the team lead 2.0 h total 14.0 h 14 / 20 = 70% of capacity by estimate. It looks comfortable.

Multiply by your overrun ratio from session 2. Your median ratio of actual to estimate turns the planned total into what the work usually takes. The difference is the overrun, and it lands on your slack before anything else does.

★ Step 3 · apply the overrunplanned 14.0 h x median ratio 1.2 = expected work 16.8 h, call it 17 h overrun = 17 - 14 = about 3 h Your numbers will differ: use your own median, not Dara's 1.2.

Add the quick asks and the time it takes to resume. Quick asks are the five- and ten-minute requests that arrive mid-task. Each one also costs a few minutes of getting back into what you were doing. Estimate both from last week: how many asks, about how long each, and a few minutes of resuming each time. Session 5 looks hard at what the research does and does not say about that resume cost; for now, a rough figure from your own week is enough.

★ Step 4 · asks and resumingDara (constructed), one week: quick asks about 12, roughly 7-8 min each = about 1.5 h resuming after each about 7-8 min = about 1.5 h real work = 17 + 1.5 + 1.5 = 20 h

Divide for your real load, and compare it with the bench. Real load is everything the week will actually ask of you, divided by the focus hours for your list. Then set it beside the session 4 bench, which runs eight modelled weeks of a constructed analyst: its rung 3 plans to 70 percent of focus hours and accepts 68.3 percent of them by estimate, and that week runs at a real load of 93.8 percent. Dara's constructed week and the bench tell the same story from two directions.

★ Step 5 · real loadreal load = real work / focus hours for the list = 20 / 20 = 100% Plan by estimate 70% -> real load 100% (Dara, constructed) Bench rung 3 68.3% by estimate -> 93.8% real load On the curve from Part 2, 100% has no ceiling: the list never drains.

Choose your plan level. Work backwards. Pick the real load you want the week to run at, then solve for how much you can plan by estimate once overruns, asks and resuming are taken out. There is no single right target: a lower one keeps waits short and dates honest, a higher one finishes more but makes everything on the list wait longer. Session 4's bench shows that trade with real model output, including what slack costs in tasks finished.

★ Step 6 · plan levelDara wants the week to run at about 80% real load: target real work 0.8 x 20 = 16 h minus asks + resume 16 - 3 = 13 h of true task time divide by ratio 1.2 13 / 1.2 = about 11 h planned by estimate Plan about 11 h of the 20 (about 55%), keep the rest as one named block, and move the 3 h that no longer fit to next week or hand back. Constructed arithmetic. Your numbers will differ.

A plan level is a choice, so say it out loud. When the team lead asks why only 11 hours of new work fit, show the arithmetic: capacity, estimates, your own overrun ratio, the asks. A number built from your own week is far easier to agree on than "I'm busy". If the same meetings eat your focus hours every week, the fix is upstream of the plan; Facilitation ↗ covers meetings that eat the day.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Compute last week's real load. Use what actually happened: hours spent on the list, quick asks and resuming, divided by the focus hours you had for it. Write the plan-by-estimate figure next to it and note the gap.
  2. Count your queue. On two different days, count the tasks sitting on your list and how many you finished. Use Little's law to estimate how many days the average task waits with you.
  3. Book one shared slack block. Take the hours you would have spread as padding, put them in one named block next week, and give it a default use for when nothing overruns.
  4. Plan next week at a level you chose. Pick a real-load target, work backwards as in step 6, and record what you moved or handed back to fit it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · For one person, what does utilisation measure?

Utilisation is a share of time, not a wait or a count. That is exactly why it can mislead: two weeks at the same utilisation can make work wait very different lengths of time, depending on how unevenly the work arrives and how much its lengths vary.

2 · On the one-server queueing curve, what happens to the average wait as utilisation goes from 80 to 90 percent?

The wait in task-lengths is rho / (1 - rho): 0.8 / 0.2 = 4 and 0.9 / 0.1 = 9. Ten points of busyness more than double the wait, and the curve keeps steepening toward 100 percent.

3 · What was Parkinson's law, as first published?

The line "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion" opens a satirical essay about bureaucracy, as reported. It is a sharp joke about organisations, not a measurement of individual slack.
