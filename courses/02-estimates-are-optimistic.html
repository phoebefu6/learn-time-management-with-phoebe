Part 1 · covers the planning fallacy

33.9 days forecast, 55.5 days taken

5 min live

In 1994 Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross asked honours students how long their thesis would take, then waited and recorded when each one actually finished. The students knew their topic, their supervisor and their own habits better than anyone. They still forecast about three weeks too short, on average, and fewer than a third finished by their own best estimate.

LiveWhat Buehler, Griffin and Ross measured3 min▶

Study 1 followed 37 psychology students in the final semester of an honours thesis course. Each gave a best estimate of when they would finish. The researchers then recorded the real finishing dates; four students had not finished when records stopped, so the averages are on the 33 who had. On average the students said 33.9 days and took 55.5, a gap of 21.6 days. Only 29.7 percent finished by the date they had called their most accurate prediction.

Two things make this result hard to wave away. The students were not guessing about a stranger's work; it was their own thesis, in their own field, with a deadline they cared about. And their forecasts were not random: predicted and actual times were strongly related across students. People knew which theses would take longer. They just put the whole set too early.

Self-studyWhere the term came from3 min▶

Buehler and colleagues credit the name "planning fallacy" to Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, in a 1979 paper titled "Intuitive prediction: Biases and corrective procedures" (TIMS Studies in Management Science, 12). Their working definition, paraphrased: a confident belief that your own project will go as planned, held even while you know most similar projects have run late.

The 1979 text itself was not read for this page; the attribution is as cited in Buehler, Griffin and Ross (1994). A later secondary summary reports that the 1979 paper was conceptual, proposing the correction rather than running experiments, which is why the 1994 studies are where the numbers come from. Part 3 returns to that correction, which looks at similar past cases instead of at the plan in front of you.

Real world

"Two days, maybe three." A familiar pattern on data teams: someone is asked how long it will take to move a recurring report onto a new data source, pictures the query rewrite, and says two days. The query is two days. Then a column turns out to mean something slightly different in the new source, the numbers do not tie out, the stakeholder wants the old and new versions side by side for a month, and the report goes live on day nine. Every surprise was ordinary. None of them was in the picture that produced "two days".

Part 2 · covers best case, worst case and confidence

Even the worst case is not safe

5 min live

The obvious fix is to ask for a range: a best case, a realistic case and a worst case, and plan on the worst. Buehler's team asked exactly that, in the same study. The worst-case forecasts were longer, as you would hope. They were still short of what actually happened, and fewer than half the students finished by their own worst case.

LiveThe worst case still missed by half3 min▶

Alongside the best estimate, students gave a best case ("if everything went as well as it possibly could") and a worst case ("if everything went as poorly as it possibly could"). The best case averaged 27.4 days and 10.8 percent finished by it. The worst case averaged 48.6 days, and only 48.7 percent finished by it. The worst case was still more than a week short of the 55.5 days the average thesis took.

Why does imagining disaster not work? One reading consistent with the study: when people picture the worst case, they still picture this project going its own way, just with more things going wrong inside the plan. What sinks real work is usually not inside the plan at all: the illness, the dataset that arrives late, the second project that lands in the same month. A worst case built from the plan inherits the plan's blind spots.

Self-studyStudy 2: other tasks, the same direction, and high confidence3 min▶

A thesis is a long, unusual project, so the team checked ordinary tasks too. In Study 2, students forecast school tasks and everyday ones. Academic tasks were predicted at 5.8 days and took 10.7, with 37.1 percent finished on time. Non-academic tasks were predicted at 5.0 days and took 9.2, with 42.5 percent on time.

The part worth sitting with is confidence. Asked how sure they were of finishing by their forecast, students said 74.1 percent for academic tasks and 69.9 percent for non-academic ones. Roughly seven in ten certainty, roughly four in ten delivery. Feeling sure is not evidence that a forecast is good; it is how a forecast feels from the inside.

Hofstadter's law, reported. Douglas Hofstadter's line from Godel, Escher, Bach (1979), as widely reported: "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law." It is a joke, not a finding, but it is a precise one: the worst-case result above is what it looks like when people do take the law into account and still come up short.

Real world

The padded estimate that still slipped. A team lead who has been burned before takes each analyst's estimate and adds 20 percent before telling the stakeholder. It helps for a while. Then a quarter arrives where the padded dates slip too, because the padding was a fixed guess about how wrong people are, not a measurement of how wrong this team is on this kind of work. A number taken from the team's own past would have said how much to add, and the build-along below does exactly that.

Part 3 · covers the outside view and its limits

The outside view fixes the forecast, not the finish

5 min live

If imagining the plan is the problem, the cure is to look somewhere else: at how similar tasks went in the past. That is the outside view, and Buehler's Study 4 tested a version of it. It worked on the forecast. It did not make anyone faster, and that distinction is the whole of tonight's honest answer.

LiveWhat the past-experience prompt did and did not do3 min▶

Study 4 had three groups forecasting the same kind of assignment. The control group simply forecast: 29.3 percent finished by their prediction. A recall group first listed past similar tasks: 38.1 percent. The recall-relevant group listed past tasks and then had to say how those past experiences bore on this forecast: 60.0 percent finished by their prediction, and the optimistic bias was removed.

Here is the disagreement to hold on to. The same study reports that the prompt "did not influence when they finished the assignment"; deadlines did that. And the forecasts were no more accurate in absolute terms: the average miss stopped leaning early, but individual misses were as wide as before. So the outside view gives you better calibration at the same speed. It makes your dates honest. It does not give you more hours, and anyone who sells it as a way to get work done sooner is claiming more than the evidence shows.

Self-studyOther people's estimates lean the other way3 min▶

Study 5 asked observers to predict how long someone else's task would take. Actors, forecasting their own task, were optimistic, with a bias of about 1.3 days early. Observers were pessimistic, about 1.7 days late, and they made more use of the person's relevant past. An outsider does not picture your plan; they picture how things like this usually go, which is the outside view without being asked for it. That is the reason for step 6 of the build-along.

Neither bias is a virtue: an observer's padding is as wrong as an actor's optimism, in the other direction. The useful move is to put the two side by side. For anything bigger than a task, a whole project with many people and dependencies, the same idea grows into reference-class forecasting, taught at project scale in Data PMO ↗. Here the reference class is the smallest one there is: your own last ten tasks.

Real world

The honest date that disappointed everyone. An analyst starts quoting dates from their own track record instead of from the plan. The dates get later, the stakeholder grumbles, and for a month the analyst feels slower. Then the stakeholder notices that every date now holds, and starts planning their own meeting around it. Nothing got done faster. What changed is that the date became something another person could build on, which is what a date is for.

Build-along

Re-estimate from your own history

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Dara has eight finished tasks with an estimate and an actual time written next to each. Those eight pairs are a private reference class, and they turn next fortnight's 41 hours into a number Dara can defend. Build yours alongside, from your own pairs; if you do not have enough yet, follow Dara's constructed numbers and fill yours in next week. Your numbers will differ.

Gather 5 to 10 pairs of estimate and actual. Start from the three pairs you recorded in session 1's homework. Add more from wherever your past lives: closed tickets with an estimate field, calendar blocks you booked against the time the work really took, or old messages where you promised a date. Pick tasks like the ones on next fortnight's list.

★ Step 1 · gather pairsOne line each: task | estimate (h) | actual (h) | source Dara (constructed): ops data pull 1.0 0.9 code review 1.0 1.0 ops data pull 2.0 2.0 dashboard fix 2.5 3.0 dashboard fix 2.0 2.5 analysis section 4.0 6.0 code review 1.0 1.5 dashboard fix 1.5 3.0

Divide actual by estimate for each pair. A ratio of 1.0 means the estimate was right; 1.5 means the task took half as long again; under 1.0 means it came in early. Ratios let you compare a one-hour task with a six-hour one on the same line.

★ Step 2 · ratiosratio = actual / estimate Dara, sorted: 0.9 1.0 1.0 1.2 1.25 1.5 1.5 2.0 Your numbers will differ. Write yours in the same sorted row.

Find your median ratio and your share at or under estimate. The median is the middle of the sorted row, so one disaster does not swamp it. Then count how many came in at or under 1.0. Compare it with the thesis students' 29.7 percent. Session 4's bench draws its overruns from a table set so that 30 percent of tasks finish within estimate, matching that share, with a far milder tail than the thesis study because its tasks are hours, not months.

★ Step 3 · median and share8 ratios -> median = average of 4th and 5th = (1.2 + 1.25) / 2 = 1.225, call it 1.2 At or under 1.0: 3 of 8 = 37.5% Thesis study: 29.7% by best estimate Session 4 bench: 30% within estimate, milder tail Dara is a little better than the students. Still over, most of the time.

Apply your median to next fortnight's list. Multiply the total of your estimates by your median ratio. This is the outside view on your own work: not a new guess about these tasks, but the record of how your guesses usually go.

★ Step 4 · apply the medianDara (constructed arithmetic): list total, next two weeks 41 h x median ratio 1.2 = expected real work about 49 h focus hours available about 40 h gap about 9 h, before any quick ask

Write each estimate as two numbers. Keep your best guess, and next to it write "if it goes the usual way", which is the guess times your median. For the lines that matter most, add a third figure from the high end of your own ratios. Quote the second number when someone else will plan around your date.

★ Step 5 · two numbers per linetask best guess usual way (x1.2) bad run (x1.5) quarterly churn analysis 6 h about 7 h 9 h exec dashboard fix 2 h about 2.5 h 3 h ops data pull 1 h about 1 h 1.5 h "Best guess six hours; if it goes the way my analyses usually go, about seven. I'll book a block for seven."

Ask a colleague to estimate one task. Pick the line you are least sure of and ask someone who knows the work, without telling them your number. Observers in Study 5 leaned late where the people doing the work leaned early, so the two numbers bracket the truth more often than either one alone.

★ Step 6 · a second opinion"Quick one: how long would you guess the churn analysis takes, start to a reviewed draft? Don't overthink it." Dara: 6 h (usual way: about 7) Teammate: 9 h Plan the block on 7, warn the team lead it could reach 9.

Keep the pairs coming. Every task you finish this week is a new pair. Add the estimate and the actual to the same sheet, and your median gets sharper each fortnight. The ratio is not a judgement of how good you are; it is a correction factor, the way a scale that reads light by a fixed amount is still a useful scale.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Finish your reference class. Get to at least eight estimate and actual pairs, compute the ratios, and write down your median and your share at or under estimate.
  2. Re-total your list. Multiply next fortnight's estimates by your median and write the new total next to your focus hours. Note the gap in hours.
  3. Quote one date the new way. The next time someone asks when something will be ready, give the "usual way" figure, say where it came from, and note how they reacted.
  4. Get one outside estimate. Ask a colleague to estimate one task before you tell them yours. When the task is done, record all three numbers: yours, theirs and the actual.
